01 / THE PROBLEM

The debt treadmill.
When tomorrow’s salary is already spent.

IN PLAIN ENGLISHOne loan may be manageable. Five payments arriving together can turn an ordinary payday into a disappearing act.

This project focuses on young adults aged 18–29 entering work, forming households and considering a first car or home. The wider issue is national: in a May 2026 DEMOSCOPE survey of 1,100 adults, 72.7% reported a current loan, 33% several loans and 46.7% repayments above half their monthly income [39]. These are self-reported adult results, not youth-specific rates.

THE CENTRAL CLAIM
Credit can turn tomorrow’s paycheque into money that has already been promised. When repayments come first, the household has less freedom over the work, time and choices that remain.

A household problem, not a personal failing

Respondents borrowed for appliances (24.2%), home repairs (18.9%) and because money ran out before payday (17.4%); 9.4% borrowed to repay another loan [39]. Debt is not simply luxury spending: some borrowing covers ordinary needs, but borrowing to reach payday can claim the next salary too.

Banks earn interest and may receive fees, but also bear funding and default costs. World Bank reports 2025 sector ROE of 28.4% and net interest income growth of 32.3% [40]; these are not consumer-loan profit figures.

Payday, in one sentence: your salary arrives; your repayments are already waiting by the door.

Who gains—and what the data can prove

At 1 October 2026, ARDFM recorded ₸26.7tn in individual loans (₸17.7tn consumer; ₸7.9tn mortgage). Banks reported ₸1.812tn net profit for January–September and 22.7% ROE [41]. These figures show market scale, not misconduct by every lender.

“Debt treadmill” is a metaphor, not literal slavery. Fixed repayments can make it harder to refuse overtime, leave a bad job or retrain. Across 29 OECD countries, high private debt is associated with weaker wage growth and greater risk of wage falls, especially for vulnerable workers [42].

When a repayment becomes a workplace constraint

A 2025 Central Bank of Brazil study links credit records to employment after mass layoffs. Workers with more unsecured or wage-linked debt were less likely to re-enter employment; collateralised debt showed a different pattern [43]. This suggests a mechanism to test, not a Kazakhstan estimate.

A loan does not itself increase tax: repayments use take-home income after deductions. Overtime may be taxed because it is earned, not because a person borrowed. The concern is that repayments absorb disposable income and narrow choices.

THE BANK’S LEDGERInterest + fees

Contracted repayments become revenue, net of the bank’s funding, operating and credit-risk costs.

THE HOUSEHOLD’S LEDGERTime + options

Repayments use take-home income that could otherwise support saving, care, mobility or a job change.

The social-science question

How do credit marketing, instalment framing, social aspirations and income insecurity combine to shape debt among young adults in Kazakhstan—and how do repayments affect disposable income, work choices and resilience to shocks?

The 70-person youth study is exploratory; DEMOSCOPE covers adults of all ages [28, 39]. A car may enable work and a home may provide security, so aspiration is not assumed to be irresponsible.

Section 1 takeaway The policy problem is not borrowing itself. It is a credit system and household budget in which future wages can be committed faster than financial resilience grows.

Where the data goes silent

No public dataset links age, bank and merchant loans, BNPL, informal debt, income, work hours and well-being. Regulated lending is measured more consistently than seller instalments [22]; youth studies are small and survey results self-reported [12, 28, 29, 39].

Not every borrower is distressed: 63.3% felt confident about their financial future [39]. A mortgage may build an asset. Risk depends on repayment load, income stability, savings and borrowing to repay older debt.

What the numbers establish:

The data establish widespread credit, material repayment costs, rapid car-lending growth and high bank profitability. They do not show that every lender abuses borrowers, every debt is harmful or all bank profit comes from consumer credit.

Section 2 takeaway The data point to a real affordability issue, but fragmented and self-reported measures mean the most vulnerable households can still disappear between datasets.

03 / CURRENT STATE OF RESEARCH

Debt is more than a number.
It changes the choices people can make.

THE QUICK READResearch points to several forces: money knowledge, how payments are advertised, social pressure and job security. No single explanation fits every borrower.

Economics, behavioural science and sociology link debt to financial capability, payment framing, status expectations and workers’ bargaining power.

Approach A — Financial capability and unequal resources

Lusardi and Mitchell define financial literacy as knowledge for informed saving and borrowing [4]. Kaiser and Menkhoff’s meta-analysis of 126 evaluations finds education can improve knowledge and some behaviours, but effects vary and are weaker when people lack room to change their finances [5]. Drexler, Fischer and Schoar find that simple rules of thumb improved some practices among Dominican micro-entrepreneurs [8]. This supports practical teaching, not the claim that information can solve low wages or high housing costs.

Approach B — Payment framing and instalment design

Using retailer data and three preregistered experiments, Maesen and Ang associate BNPL with a 9-point rise in purchase incidence and about 10% higher purchase amounts; instalments lowered perceived financial constraints [24]. A Central Bank of Ireland experiment with 3,000 consumers found BNPL users spent 4.39% more than debit-card users. Risk disclosure improved comprehension by 10%, but did not significantly reduce use or spending [25]. These are not Kazakhstan market estimates.

Approach C — Status, identity and everyday consumption

González-López links consumer credit with ordinary consumption, class identity and belonging [26]. A car or apartment may signal independence, adulthood or security as well as provide practical use. Kazakhstan evidence is suggestive: Zholdasbekova surveyed 70 young adults aged 18–25; Burakanova and colleagues study generational differences in conspicuous consumption [28, 29]. The MNU report discusses student BNPL use [12]. These sources cannot show how many young people borrow mainly for status.

Approach D — Debt and the labour market

Denk and Fialho analyse household data from 29 OECD countries and associate high private debt with weaker wage growth and greater risk of wage falls, especially for vulnerable workers [42]. A 2025 Banco Central do Brasil paper links credit data to employment after mass layoffs: workers with more unsecured or wage-linked debt were less likely to re-enter employment, while collateralised debt differed [43]. This is not proof of the same effect in Kazakhstan; it shows why work choices belong in the question.

Approach E — Education can help, but outcomes can surprise

Bruhn and colleagues’ large Brazilian high-school programme improved financial knowledge and some saving and budgeting behaviours, but also increased expensive consumer-credit use [10]. Better knowledge does not guarantee safer borrowing. A later meta-analysis finds positive average effects on knowledge and downstream behaviours, but size and durability vary [7]. Tenge Sense must test decisions, not only quiz scores.

Approach F — What AI has and has not shown

Ayberkin and Yirik’s one-week AI financial-literacy pilot with 32 Turkish university students reported pre/post gains, but had no control group and repeated test items [13]. A Reach Alliance / Singapore Management University conversational-AI prototype was co-designed with migrant workers but did not achieve sustained use [14]. Erica and StepChange demonstrate scale, not debt reduction [20, 30].

The gap is a lack of youth-focused evidence on how bank loans, BNPL, merchant instalments and mortgages combine in one household budget, and whether repayments affect work or well-being. No local evidence yet shows that an AI tutor reduces over-indebtedness. Tenge Sense tests whether simulations improve decisions without encouraging more borrowing.

Section 3 takeaway Financial education can help, but it cannot substitute for income security, transparent credit terms or responsible lending. Debt’s effect on work and choice deserves equal attention.

04 / THE AI-POWERED POLICY SOLUTIONIMPLEMENTATION PLAN

Tenge Sense AI
Learn it. Simulate it. Decide better.

THE QUICK READA 12-week school programme powered by three AI tools: a financial-life simulator, an advertisement investigator and a personalised tutor. Teachers remain in charge; AI makes practice adaptive and repeatable.

How the AI system works

01

AI financial-life simulator

Builds fictional household scenarios covering salary, rent, transport, food, savings, instalments and unexpected costs. It adapts later decisions and explains consequences.

02

AI advertisement investigator

Extracts visible payments, ГЭСВ, down-payment, term and fees from public ads. It flags absent or unclear details and persuasive framing; a human checks every flag.

03

AI personal tutor

Explains concepts in Kazakh and Russian using verified materials, diagnoses misconceptions with practice questions and adapts difficulty. It cites sources and admits uncertainty.

04

AI teacher copilot

Drafts exercises and role-plays and summarises common class errors. Teachers approve materials; individual chats are not used to rank students.

Each week has a 45–60-minute teacher-led class and optional guided AI practice. The 12-week course sits inside a 12-month pilot for preparation, delivery and follow-up.

Who operates it?

The Ministry of Education approves school delivery. The National Bank and Өрлеу verify financial content and support teachers; the financial regulator reviews consumer-rights material. Universities design scenarios and evaluate results. Qaryzsyz Qogam provides referral routes. A selected technology partner builds and maintains the prototype. Lenders do not market products in class.

Why use AI—and what evidence supports the design?

AI generates scenarios, adapts explanations and extracts ad terms. Bank of America’s Erica is a virtual assistant built into digital banking; the bank reported 3.2bn cumulative interactions by March 2026 [20]. StepChange uses virtual agents and digital tools to route routine debt-advice requests and support advisers; its technology provider reports capacity equal to 40 advisers and 93% of clients choosing self-guided journeys [30]. These reach and capacity figures do not prove reduced debt; human advisers remain essential.

Budget and cost dimensions

The 12-month, 20-school pilot is estimated at ₸15–20m. The ₸15m planning case allocates ₸4m to platform/integrations, ₸2.5m to verified Kazakh/Russian content, ₸3m to AI functions, ₸2m to teacher training, ₸1.5m to security/legal review and ₸2m to evaluation/support. It is not an approved budget or supplier quote. Existing teachers, classrooms and partner expertise are in-kind; usage, hosting, devices and integrations may push costs toward ₸20m or more.

Economic: a public pilot budget funds the platform, content, support and evaluation; students pay nothing. Reuse an existing model. Labour: teachers, reviewers, translators, engineers, moderators and evaluators remain essential; annotation, moderation and maintenance are paid work. Political and governance: assign a public owner, document vendor duties, audit outputs and offer a complaint route. Kazakhstan’s AI Law requires transparency and risk management [17]. Any personal learning data must be stored in Kazakhstan under the Personal Data Law [18]. Data ethics: use fictional scenarios and pseudonymous learning records; do not collect IINs, family income, debt balances or bank credentials. Obtain appropriate consent, limit access and delete records when no longer needed. Environmental: cap the pilot at 1,000 students × 10 text interactions weekly × 12 weeks (120,000 prompts). Google’s 2025 Gemini benchmark (0.24 Wh and 0.26 mL water per median text prompt) implies about 28.8 kWh and 31 L for that volume [15]—not a measurement of this service; it excludes development, image analysis and hosting. KEGOC reports 2025 generation of 74.4% thermal, 11.0% gas, 8.5% hydro and 6.1% solar, wind and bio [16]. Use text-first models and require providers to report actual energy and water use.

Risk controls

AI can hallucinate, misread ads or omit fees. Show extracted terms with source locations; mark missing fields “not visible” instead of guessing. Ad flags are leads for review, not legal findings. The tutor uses approved content, refuses personal credit decisions and refers sensitive questions to people. Paper alternatives remain available.

How success will be measured

Assess students before, after and three months after the course. Test total repayment, ГЭСВ, ad analysis, pressure tactics and budgeting. Audit AI accuracy and unsafe answers; track teacher workload and access. Where feasible, compare with similar schools receiving the programme later. The pilot tests learning, not national debt reduction.

Verdict: Tenge Sense AI should be a teacher-led, AI-powered financial decision simulator for schools—not an AI that tells people which loans to take. Its promise is safer practice before real financial commitments, with evidence collected before any national expansion.

Section 4 takeaway Use AI for what it does well: personalised practice, scenario generation and pattern detection. Keep people responsible for teaching, verification and financial decisions.

AI TRANSPARENCY STATEMENTPROJECT DISCLOSURE

What AI did.
What it did not.

I first framed the topic as general financial illiteracy, then narrowed it to household over-indebtedness, social pressure and the way multiple repayments can limit future choices. I accepted “debt treadmill” as a clear metaphor, but rejected the literal claim that borrowers are slaves. I also rejected claims that every borrower is irresponsible or every bank deliberately exploits customers.

AI assisted with research discovery, summarising sources, drafting, editing, data presentation and coding the website and calculator. I checked key claims against official Kazakhstan sources, including the National Bank, ARDFM, Bureau of National Statistics and Government reports. I kept survey limits visible and distinguished sector-level indicators from individual borrower outcomes. International research on debt and work is not proof about every worker in Kazakhstan.

I changed the proposed solution after identifying that Kazakhstan already has the Qaryzsyz Qogam project and government commitments to financial-literacy education in schools and universities. I rejected the idea that a generic chatbot or standalone website would solve over-indebtedness. The revised proposal is a teacher-led, three-month school programme in which AI has defined roles: a fictional financial-life simulator, an advertisement investigator that flags questions for human review, a Kazakh/Russian personal tutor using verified materials, and a teacher copilot. Existing public institutions and partners provide oversight and referrals. The AI system is a proposed pilot design, not a live feature of this static website.

The calculator uses deterministic arithmetic, not AI, and this static website is not connected to a live AI model. The AI simulator, tutor, advertisement investigator and teacher copilot described in the proposal are planned components, not currently deployed services. The proposed system would use fictional student scenarios and would not access bank accounts, IINs or credit histories. AI-generated explanations and advertisement flags would require expert review. I remain responsible for checking all sources, calculations and conclusions. The National Bank’s macro indicators are not individual borrower ratios. DEMOSCOPE figures are self-reported, cover adults 18+ rather than only young people, and have a 10.6% response rate. The World Bank’s debt-to-wages ratio, National Bank’s debt-service/disposable-income ratio, BNS cash-expenditure share and DEMOSCOPE’s repayment/income responses use different denominators and must not be combined. Bank profitability measures cover the sector, not just consumer lending, and do not establish lender intent. Car-loan originations (annual flow) are distinguished from portfolio balances (outstanding stock). The 70-person youth survey is exploratory; BNPL experiments are not Kazakhstan estimates. AI implementation metrics from StepChange and Erica are company/vendor-reported, not causal evaluations of debt reduction. The proposed policy programme is designed to use existing public institutions; any additional implementation budget requires costing by the responsible agencies.
